Göppingen Gö 1 Wolf

A 1935 German training glider, printed in PLA and assembled into a flying RC aircraft.

Credit where it belongs

The Göppingen Gö 1 Wolf is a single-seat training glider first flown in Germany in 1935. Eclipson adapted it as a 3D-printable radio-control model and produced the part files. The aircraft design is theirs, not mine.

What follows is the build — which is the part worth showing, because printing a functional airframe is a genuinely different problem from printing a desk ornament.

Why it is harder than it looks

An aircraft is the least forgiving thing you can 3D print. Every gram of unnecessary material is a gram the wing has to lift, and a part that would be perfectly serviceable on a desk is dead weight on a wing.

Eclipson publishes this model in several build categories. I printed Category B2, the standard PLA variant — the least exotic option and, for that reason, the one with the least margin. Lighter foaming filaments exist and buy you slack on the mass budget; plain PLA does not. The prints have to come off the bed dimensionally right, because there is nothing to compensate with later.

The build

48 parts, printed in PLA and sliced in Bambu Studio. At that part count the tolerances stop being per-part and start being cumulative:

  • Wing and fuselage sections that have to key into each other along the length of the airframe
  • Control surfaces sized to hinge cleanly without binding or leaving slop
  • Mounts and fittings that have to land on the hardware they were drawn around
  • Anything warped or over-extruded compounds down the assembly rather than staying local to one part

Assembly

Beyond printing: hinging and linking the ailerons and elevator, mounting servos and setting linkage geometry so the control throws are symmetric, fitting the motor mount and landing gear, and balancing the airframe to the centre of gravity the design calls for. An aircraft that is nose-heavy is sluggish; tail-heavy and it is uncontrollable. That number is not optional.

Why it is on this site

This is fabrication, not design, and it is listed as such. It earns its place because it is the clearest evidence of the 3D printing side of what I do — a large part count, tolerances that have to hold across the whole assembly, and an outcome that either works or falls out of the sky.

Most 3D printing portfolios show things that sit on a shelf. This one had to fly.
